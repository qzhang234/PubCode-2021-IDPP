Dear Editors:

Please consider the enclosed Research Report as a Direct Submission to
\textit{PNAS}: ``Dynamic Arrest in an Elastin-like Protein Polymer Follows a
Single Pathway Paced by a Sub-Kelvin Temperature Scale.''

\textbf{Corresponding authors.} Qingteng Zhang
\mbox{(\href{mailto:qzhang234@anl.gov}{qzhang234@anl.gov})}, Arvind Ramanathan
\mbox{(\href{mailto:ramanathana@anl.gov}{ramanathana@anl.gov})}, and Gyorgy Babnigg
\mbox{(\href{mailto:gbabnigg@anl.gov}{gbabnigg@anl.gov})}.

\textbf{Classification.} Physical Sciences: Applied Physical Sciences.
Secondary: Biophysics and Computational Biology.

\textbf{Significance.} Whether a soft or biological material has stopped
flowing is usually inferred from its structure: a scattering profile that has
grown enough is read as a gel. We show that this inference can fail. In the
sequence-defined elastin-like polypeptide (VPAVG)$_{30}$, four loadings held
between 28.00 and 28.75~$^\circ$C all assemble, but only those above
28.00~$^\circ$C arrest within the hold, and at the onset of arrest the profile
lies within 18\% at every $Q$ of the 28.00~$^\circ$C profile, which is still
mobile after 6500~s. A quarter of a degree decides it, not an amount of
structure. Arrest also reaches the largest length scales first, and it advances
by converting material out of the mobile population rather than by slowing it:
the fast-decay amplitude falls from 0.80 to 0.07 at 175~nm within an hour while
the fitted diffusion coefficient varies by a factor of two.

\textbf{Novelty.} One rescaling of the waiting time, $\exp(\Delta T/T_s)$,
collapses all four holds onto a single master curve with $T_s=0.22\pm0.02$~K,
and the same $T_s$ to within 13\% in every one of the five $Q$ bins. That is a
stronger statement than a rate: the sample passes through the same sequence of
dynamical states at every temperature and every length scale measured, and
temperature sets only the pace. A scaling temperature of a few tenths of a
kelvin is far too sharp for a single contact, which places the rate-limiting
step at the concerted formation of a junction in which many contacts close
together.

That scaling framework is our own. One of us introduced it for thermoreversible
colloidal gels [Q. Zhang \textit{et al.}, \textit{Phys. Rev. Lett.}
\textbf{119}, 178006 (2017)], where it returns $T_s=0.33$ and 0.45~K for two
particle sizes [D. Bahadur \textit{et al.}, \textit{J. Chem. Phys.}
\textbf{151}, 104902 (2019)]. The present work carries it from colloids to a
protein polymer and finds the same cooperative regime. We cite the agreement
with the nanoparticle-gel phenomenology rather than claiming it as new; what is
new is that temperature and structure are separable in a protein network, and
that one time scale governs every length scale we can measure.

\textbf{Why it needed this measurement.} The fast relaxation is
millisecond-scale and falls at or below the shortest delay a conventional
kilohertz acquisition reaches. The decomposition rests on framing X-ray speckle
at 50~kHz with the XSPA-500k at APS 8-ID-I; at conventional speeds neither the
length-scale ordering nor the scaling collapse is visible at all.

\textbf{Limits, stated plainly.} The 1.6~s maximum delay bounds a fraction
arrested on that scale rather than an infinite-time nonergodicity factor. The
scaling rests on three arresting temperatures spanning half a degree, the
collapse is approximate rather than exact, and $T_s$ drifts by 13\% across the
five $Q$ bins.

\textbf{Companion manuscript.} A separate manuscript, in preparation from the
same construct, reports the structural side: a thermally reversible transition
to a hierarchical assembly carrying $\beta$-sheet-like order, and the
dependence of that reversibility on chain length. The two were originally one
Letter and were separated because the dynamical result stands on its own and
the structural result belongs with the chain-length series. There is no
overlap of figures or data between them. We are happy to supply the companion
manuscript on request.
