\begin{abstract}
Small-angle X-ray photon correlation spectroscopy (SA-XPCS) of the sequence-defined elastin-like polypeptide \VPAVG{} separates dynamic arrest from the assembly that SAXS records. Four loadings held between 28.00 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius} all assemble, yet only those above \SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius} arrest within the hold, and SAXS cannot tell their states apart: nearly the same profile is mobile at \SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius} and arresting a quarter of a degree higher. At \SI{28.50}{\degreeCelsius} the fast-decay amplitude of a two-step correlation function falls from 0.80 to 0.07 at \SI{175}{\nano\meter} within an hour, and less far at every smaller scale down to \SI{83}{\nano\meter}. The time to half arrest shortens fivefold over \SI{0.5}{\degreeCelsius}, and rescaling the waiting time by $\exp(\Delta T/T_s)$ collapses all four onto one master curve with $T_s=0.22\pm0.02$~K at every length scale measured. A scaling temperature this sharp cannot be set by a single contact, placing arrest behind a step in which many contacts close together.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Significance Statement}
Whether a soft material has solidified is usually inferred from its structure: a scattering pattern that has grown enough is read as a gel. We show that this inference can fail. In a designed protein polymer, samples held within a degree of one another develop nearly the same structure, yet only the warmer ones stop moving. Seeing this needed X-ray speckle framed at fifty thousand images per second, because the motion reporting on arrest is millisecond-scale, below what conventional detectors reach. Arrest spreads region by region, largest scales first, and one rescaling of waiting time collapses every sample onto a single pathway whose temperature scale is a fifth of a degree, too sharp for single contacts to set.

\section*{Introduction}
Protein polymers that assemble on heating offer a route to materials whose behavior is written into a gene. Elastin-like polypeptides (ELPs) are the best developed of them, because their sequence, molecular weight, and lower critical solution temperature (LCST) behavior can be programmed recombinantly.\cite{Meyer2004-zb,Amiram2011-la,Quiroz2015-mh,Varanko2020-ek,Saha2020-jg} Above the LCST, ELPs undergo hydrophobic desolvation and can form coacervates or hierarchical assemblies depending on sequence, concentration, and thermal history.\cite{Varanko2020-ek,Saha2020-jg,Sing2017-zq} In particular, alanine-containing ELPs can form stiff, thermoresponsive gels through arrested phase separation.\cite{Glassman2015-cw,Glassman2016-pq} Although these studies establish the arrested state from time-averaged scattering and rheology, how an assembly evolves toward arrest, and what paces it, remains elusive.

Whether an assembled state is still evolving is a question for a dynamical measurement. In colloidal gels, microscopic relaxation continues to evolve after the time-averaged structure has nearly stopped changing, a nonequilibrium process termed physical aging.\cite{Bahadur2019-ln,Chen2023-ho} Related heterogeneous relaxation has been resolved during protein liquid--liquid phase separation,\cite{Girelli2021-zz} protein gelation,\cite{Begam2021-fh} and cage relaxation in crowded protein solutions.\cite{Chushkin2022-bw} A protein-rich morphology can likewise keep reorganizing while its SAXS profile changes modestly, through junction exchange, whose kinetics set the network relaxation time in associative networks built from this same pentapeptide family,\cite{Sing2018-er} and through the residual coarsening that follows arrested phase separation.\cite{Tanaka2000-qq,Zhang2013-ja} A dynamical measurement can also separate two routes to arrest. If the assembly stiffens as a whole, relaxation times lengthen continuously. If it instead converts region by region, the material that is still mobile keeps its relaxation rate while its share of the scattering falls. A bulk measurement averages the two together. Small-angle X-ray photon correlation spectroscopy (SA-XPCS) probes the evolution directly through speckle decorrelation at the same low-$Q$ length scales sampled by SAXS.

Here, isothermal SA-XPCS resolves that evolution in concentrated aqueous \VPAVG, an ELP built from 30 repeats of the pentapeptide valine-proline-alanine-valine-glycine. Four loadings were held between 28.00 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}. All four assemble, yet only those above \SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius} arrest within the hold, and their correlation functions show a two-step decay whose fast fraction falls with elapsed time, first at the largest length scales. Arrest is set by temperature rather than by the structure SAXS records, one rescaling of time carries all four holds onto a single curve, and the temperature scale of that rescaling is too sharp for any single contact to set.

To resolve mesoscale arrest isothermally, four loadings were each held at \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius} for more than \SI{30}{\minute}, warmed to \SI{20}{\degreeCelsius}, then taken at \SI{0.1}{\degreeCelsius\per\minute} to a final temperature between 28.00 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius} and held there. The waiting time $t_w$ starts at the first acquisition with the calibrated resistance-temperature detector (RTD) at the cell reading the target. Figure~\ref{fig:xpcs} shows the loading held at \SI{28.50}{\degreeCelsius}; the other three are in SI Figures~\ref{SI-fig:S7}, \ref{SI-fig:S8} and \ref{SI-fig:S10}, and Figure~\ref{fig:arrest} puts all four on one axis. Each $t_w$ labels a group of averaged consecutive acquisitions at previously unexposed positions (SI Section~\ref{SI-sec:tdepdata}). The SAXS intensity starts close to the \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius} reference of the same loading and grows fourfold over the hold at low $Q$, staying broad and monotonic with no peak emerging (Figure~\ref{fig:xpcs}a), so the assembly gains density contrast without settling on a single characteristic domain size. Correlation functions are fitted in the five lowest $Q$ bins, 0.00359 to 0.00756~\Ainv{} or 175 to \SI{83}{\nano\meter}; above them the scattered intensity has fallen by more than an order of magnitude and $g_2$ is dominated by counting noise.

The intensity autocorrelation function $g_2$ for a single SA-XPCS acquisition is defined as:
\begin{equation}
 g_2(Q,\Delta t)=\frac{\langle I(Q,t)I(Q,t+\Delta t)\rangle_t}{\langle I(Q,t)\rangle_t^2},
 \label{eq:g2}
\end{equation}
where $I(Q,t)$ is the coherent scattering intensity and $\Delta t$ is the delay time. Through the Siegert relation, $g_2$ reports the relaxation of density fluctuations at length scale $2\pi/Q$. At the largest length scale the correlation functions show a two-step decay: a millisecond-scale relaxation followed by an apparent plateau that persists through the \SI{2}{\second} acquisition (Figure~\ref{fig:xpcs}b), the fast step being resolved only by the \SI{50}{\kilo\hertz} framing of the XSPA-500k.\cite{Nakaye2021-oc} 

As $t_w$ elapses over repeated acquisitions, the amplitude of the first decay decreases and the plateau rises. Such two-step relaxation and growth of a slow component are characteristic of heterogeneous kinetic arrest in protein and colloidal gels.\cite{Begam2021-fh,Bahadur2019-ln,Chen2020-fn} The two-step decays in $g_2$ were parameterized by:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 g_2(Q,\Delta t,t_w)={}&1+\beta\left|g_1(Q,\Delta t,t_w)\right|^{2},\\
 g_1(Q,\Delta t,t_w)={}&f(Q,t_w)\exp\!\left[-\left(D_0(t_w)\,Q^{2}\,\Delta t\right)^{p_{\mathrm{fast}}}\right]\\
 &+\left[1-f(Q,t_w)\right]\exp\!\left[-\left(\frac{\Delta t}{\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}(Q,t_w)}\right)^{p_{\mathrm{slow}}(t_w)}\right],
\end{aligned}
 \label{eq:twomode}
\end{equation}
The first line is the Siegert relation, in which $g_1$ is the normalized intermediate scattering function and $\beta$ is the instrumental speckle contrast, measured on a static reference and held fixed (SI Section~\ref{SI-sec:tdepbg}). The second line is the two-mode model, where $f$ is the fitted fast fraction and $\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}$ an effective slow relaxation time. The effective fast relaxation time is replaced by $1/(D_0Q^{2})$ in the fast term, where $D_0$ is a fitted diffusion coefficient. That form carries a physical claim: the population that is still mobile moves diffusively, so a density fluctuation of wavelength $2\pi/Q$ relaxes in the time a scatterer needs to diffuse across it and the relaxation rate grows as $Q^{2}$. Released from the constraint the fitted exponent has a median of $-2.5$, and the collapse of Figure~\ref{fig:arrest} survives releasing it (SI Section~\ref{SI-sec:xpcsfit}). The stretching exponents $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$ and $p_{\mathrm{slow}}$ describe the breadth of the relaxation-time distributions; both are shared across the five $Q$ bins of a group and both are free from one group to the next. A stretched correlation function can still be diffusive as long as $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$ does not depend on $Q$ and the characteristic time scales with $Q^{-2}$. The rate of the fast mode is reported as $D_0$ itself, a parameter of the fit rather than a quantity derived from it. $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$ falls from about 1 while a hold is still mobile to about 0.2 once it has arrested, so a single value shared along a hold is rejected by an $F$ test at $p<10^{-16}$ in every hold that arrests. Holding $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$ at 1, which makes the fast mode a single exponential, describes the one hold that stays mobile but not the three that arrest, where the mean reduced $\chi^2$ rises from 1.6 to between 7 and 10. The breadth of the fast relaxation is therefore a measured feature of arrest rather than a fitting convenience. A stretching exponent below 1 means the fast term is a distribution of relaxation times rather than a single rate, and the lower it falls the wider that distribution: the delay interval over which the fast term decays from 90 to 10\% of its amplitude is $(\ln 10/\ln\tfrac{10}{9})^{1/p_{\mathrm{fast}}}$, which widens from 1.3 decades at $p_{\mathrm{fast}}=1$ to 6.7 at 0.2. Late in a hold, the material that is still mobile relaxes across a very wide spread of time scales. $\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}$ exceeds the acquisition window, so it is an extrapolated descriptor; the primary quantities are $f$ and $D_0$.

Figure~\ref{fig:xpcs}c shows the fitted fast fraction falling at every measured $Q$ over the hold. At $t_w=\SI{6}{\second}$, $f$ is 0.80 at $Q=0.00359$~\Ainv{} (\SI{175}{\nano\meter}); by \SI{3560}{\second} that bin has fallen to 0.07, so the complementary arrested fraction $1-f$ has gone from 0.20 to 0.93 in under an hour. At every analyzed time $f$ also increases monotonically with $Q$, in all seven groups, so arrest develops first and most strongly at the largest length scale probed, \SI{175}{\nano\meter}, and progressively less so toward \SI{83}{\nano\meter}. That ordering holds within each group, so it reflects length scale rather than the trend with $t_w$, and it ties the growing low-$Q$ SAXS structure to the arrest fraction measured on the very same frames. Note that XPCS does not locate the arrested regions, and the scattering contributions are weighted by structure and contrast, therefore $1-f$ is not a geometric volume fraction.

Figure~\ref{fig:xpcs}c also shows that what survives in the mobile population keeps moving. While the sample is still largely mobile, $D_0$ falls from 8.0 to $2.3\times10^{7}$~\AA$^2$\,s$^{-1}$. Through the rest of the hold it varies between 0.9 and $1.7\times10^{7}$~\AA$^2$\,s$^{-1}$ while $1-f$ climbs from 0.46 to 0.93. Progressive stiffening would look different: as regions tightened, their density fluctuations would relax ever more slowly and would stop relaxing diffusively. Instead the surviving fraction keeps a diffusive $Q^{-2}$ relaxation at a coefficient that varies by a factor of two through arrest while $f$ falls by more than a factor of ten. The amplitude changes much further than the rate and does so monotonically, so at this stage of arrest it is the amplitude that carries the change. The same pairing of a plateauing fast time scale with a declining fast amplitude was found in a thermoreversible nanoparticle gel,\cite{Bahadur2019-ln} and the dynamic heterogeneity of such gels is established by confocal microscopy and simulation.\cite{Royall2008-uq, Zhang2013-ja}

The fast amplitude and the fast rate are not equally well determined late in a hold: $f$ is set by the plateau height and stays measured, whereas any fast-mode time scale needs a resolved decay and is lost once $f$ falls to a few percent. $\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}$ lies beyond the acquisition window and is not used for scaling. Fresh material at every acquisition keeps cumulative dose from tracking $t_w$, so the trend in $f$ cannot be a dose artifact, and a control at 23--41 times this flux shows no change in $g_2$ with dose rate (Figure~\ref{SI-fig:S6}).

The other three loadings show what sets the arrest (Figure~\ref{fig:arrest}, SI Section~\ref{SI-sec:tdep} and Figures~\ref{SI-fig:S7}, \ref{SI-fig:S8} and \ref{SI-fig:S10}). All four assemble, their low-$Q$ intensity rising on the ramp and again during the hold, but they arrest at very different rates: the time for $f$ at the largest length scale to fall by half shortens fivefold between 28.25 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}. SAXS cannot tell these states apart. At the onset of arrest at 28.25 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}, $I(Q)$ lies within 18\% of the final \SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius} profile at every $Q$, and that profile is still mobile after \SI{6500}{\second}. Arrest is therefore a step distinct from assembly, set by temperature rather than by the structure that SAXS records.

One time scale describes all four. Rescaling the waiting time by $\exp[(T-T_0)/T_s]$ collapses the four $f(t_w)$ curves onto a single master curve (Figure~\ref{fig:arrest}b), with $T_s=0.22\pm0.02$~K, the mean over the five $Q$ bins and the bootstrap uncertainty of collapsing one bin on its own. Collapsed one bin at a time it reads 0.21 to 0.24~K across the five $Q$ values, from 175 to \SI{83}{\nano\meter} (Figure~\ref{SI-fig:S11}i). That says more than that warmer is faster: the sample passes through the same sequence of dynamical states at every temperature and at every length scale measured, and temperature sets only the pace. The \SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius} loading, which never arrests inside its \SI{6500}{\second} hold, is not an exception but the same curve seen too early, the scaling placing its half-arrest near \SI{15600}{\second}. A scaling temperature of a few tenths of a kelvin is itself the signature of a cooperative step. The same collapse was introduced for thermoreversible colloidal gels, where the formation rate varies with quench depth far more steeply than the pair attraction alone allows, and that steepness was attributed to the cooperative bonding needed to build locally favored structures.\cite{Zhang2017-ip,Chen2023-ho} Applied to that system it returns $T_s=0.33$ and 0.45~K for two particle sizes, corresponding to local structures held by roughly twenty bonds.\cite{Bahadur2019-ln} A single contact cannot produce such steepness: one bond's energy changes far too little over half a degree. The temperature sensitivity of an individual contact here is not independently calibrated, so no bond number is extracted, but arrest being gated by something that must form many contacts at once is what the scaling requires.

\section*{Discussion}
This changes what is understood to pace the assembly. Above the LCST, ELP assembly is driven by hydrophobic desolvation, and desolvation is well under way before any hold begins: the scattered intensity has already grown two- to fourfold by \SI{27.5}{\degreeCelsius} on the ramp (SI Section~\ref{SI-sec:tdep}). What the scaling adds is that desolvation is not the step that sets the clock. The equilibrium of a single contact shifts very little over half a degree, yet half a degree changes the rate of arrest by a factor of five, so the rate-limiting event is the concerted formation of a junction in which many contacts close together. The two dynamical observables describe that event from opposite sides. $T_s$ measures how steeply its rate depends on temperature. $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$, falling to about 0.2, measures how widely the relaxation times of the material that has not yet converted are spread, which is what a population distributed across many stages of junction assembly produces. Read together they place arrest as a region-by-region conversion, each region waiting on a step that many contacts must complete at once.

The same cooperativity carries to the reverse direction. A junction that needs many contacts to form needs many to break before it comes apart, which is the condition for a thermal transition that is sharp and that repeats. It also sets a practical scale. At $T_s=0.22$~K, holding this sequence a quarter of a degree warmer shortens the time to arrest by about a factor of three, so the assembly rate of an ELP gel is adjustable over orders of magnitude by sub-degree temperature control, with the arrested state reached along the same pathway at every setting.

The growth of low-$Q$ SAXS intensity and the decline of the fast fraction are two aspects of network formation, and the temperature series shows that they are distinct. Their sequence, assembly first and arrest after, is consistent with network-forming arrested phase separation in a dynamically asymmetric protein--solvent mixture, where concentration fluctuations build a connected dense phase and viscoelastic constraints suppress relaxation.\cite{Tanaka2000-qq,Girelli2021-zz,Begam2021-fh,Lu2008-nature} Related ELPs form bicontinuous networks with stress-relaxation times beyond $10^3$~s.\cite{Glassman2015-cw,Glassman2016-pq} Arrest that empties the mobile population rather than slowing it is what cooperative junctions produce, a region leaving that population once enough contacts form together. Cooperative assembly of that kind is familiar in protein systems, which clear a nucleation barrier before growth begins,\cite{Ferrone1999-me} accelerate once started,\cite{Knowles2009-science} and extend by templated addition onto a self-complementary interface.\cite{Sawaya2007-nature}

The route to arrest matters for what a partially arrested network can do. A medium that stiffens throughout slows every solute within it. One that converts region by region keeps pockets that are not merely less viscous but still diffusive, so transport should follow the connectivity of those pockets rather than a bulk viscosity, and release should hold and then fall at a threshold rather than tail off. Because the arrested fraction evolves in time and can be measured, a network interrupted at a chosen waiting time carries a defined fraction, a handle that neither composition nor the SAXS profile gives. The same question is open for biomolecular condensates, whose maturation from liquid to solid governs their function and their role in aggregation.\cite{Banani2017-bb,Shin2017-hi} If maturation proceeds the same way, a partially matured condensate keeps interior regions that still exchange, and the liquid-to-solid transition is a connectivity threshold rather than a gradual drift. Condensates are too small and too dilute for the measurement reported here; a concentrated, sequence-defined mimic is not, and in this one arrest is local.

Two limitations bound the interpretation, and each points to the measurement that would lift it. First, the \SIrange{2e-5}{1.6}{\second} delay range quantifies the fraction arrested on that scale rather than the infinite-time nonergodicity factor, for which linear shear rheology on a scaled-up preparation is the complement. Second, the scaling rests on three arresting temperatures spanning half a degree, and the collapse is approximate: rescaling brings the loadings threefold closer together but not to within the measurement uncertainty, and $T_s$ drifts by 13\% across the five $Q$ bins. More temperatures would settle both. $T_0$ is a convention and not a measured transition temperature, so no reference time is quoted from it.

Subject to those constraints, the measurements give one picture. SA-XPCS resolves what SAXS cannot: whether an assembly has arrested. Nearly the same mesoscale structure is mobile at \SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius} and arresting a quarter of a degree higher, arrest reaches the largest length scales first, and once under way it advances by converting mobile material rather than by slowing it. One rescaling of time carries every hold onto the same curve, so temperature sets the pace of a single pathway. A protein network can therefore arrest region by region, on a clock that a quarter of a degree resets, which makes the arrested fraction itself a quantity a preparation can be stopped at rather than a state it either has or has not reached.

\section*{Materials and Methods}
The Sanger-verified construct encoded an N-terminal His-tagged \VPAVG{} protein (complete sequence in the Supporting Information). Recombinant \VPAVG{} was expressed in \textit{E. coli}, purified by immobilized metal-affinity chromatography, and buffer exchanged into \SI{20}{\milli\mole\per\liter} HEPES, \SI{250}{\milli\mole\per\liter} NaCl, and \SI{2}{\milli\mole\per\liter} DTT at pH 8. Expression, solubility, and purity were monitored by SDS-PAGE (Figure~\ref{SI-fig:S1}b). Protein concentration was determined from $A_{280}$ using the sequence-specific extinction coefficient ($\varepsilon_{280}=1490$~M$^{-1}$\,cm$^{-1}$, 15{,}461~Da construct); the final concentration was approximately \SI{100}{\milli\gram\per\milli\liter}.

SA-XPCS was performed at APS 8-ID-I with a \SI{10.94}{\kilo\electronvolt}, $10\times10~\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}$ partially coherent beam and a Rigaku XSPA-500k detector \SI{7.8}{\meter} downstream. Each acquisition contained 100,000 frames at \SI{50}{\kilo\hertz} (\SI{20}{\micro\second} per frame, \SI{2}{\second} total), and the four holds were measured in one beamtime in a single cell block. Further acquisition, fitting and radiation-control details are in the Supporting Information.
