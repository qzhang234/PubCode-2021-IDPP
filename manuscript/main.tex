\begin{abstract}
Many proteins assemble into dense phases that then lose their mobility, as when a condensate hardens or a protein gel sets. Assembly and arrest are usually followed with different tools, so whether arrest tracks structure, and what sets its pace, have been difficult to establish. Small-angle X-ray photon correlation spectroscopy (SA-XPCS) records both structure and dynamics on the same detector frames. We apply it to the elastin-like polypeptide \VPAVG{} in isothermal experiments at four temperatures between 28.00 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}. The protein assembles at all four temperatures. Its correlation functions decay in two steps, and the amplitude of the fast step falls as the sample arrests. At \SI{28.50}{\degreeCelsius}, this amplitude drops from 0.80 to 0.07 at \SI{175}{\nano\meter} within an hour, and less far at every smaller length scale down to \SI{83}{\nano\meter}. One rescaling of the waiting time, with a scaling temperature $T_s=0.24\pm0.02$~K, collapses the arrest at all four temperatures onto a single master curve. Arrest thus speeds up by about a factor of three for every quarter of a degree of heating. The low-$Q$ SAXS intensity follows the same clock. Arrest nonetheless trails assembly: every sample that arrests is half arrested at nearly the same low-$Q$ intensity, after most of its low-$Q$ scattering has developed. The steepness of the clock corresponds to an apparent activation energy more than a thousand times the thermal energy. It points to a rate-limiting step in which many contacts close together.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Significance Statement}
Many proteins assemble into dense phases that later stop moving, as when a condensate hardens or a gel sets. Structure and motion are usually measured separately, so whether the loss of motion tracks the growth of structure has been hard to tell. We record both at once in a designed protein polymer with fast X-ray scattering. Both run on one clock, and the motion stops after most of the structure has formed. The clock runs about three times faster for every quarter of a degree of heating, so a fraction of a degree can determine when this protein material sets. This steepness points to a rate-limiting step in which many molecular contacts form together.


\section*{Introduction}
Many proteins assemble into dense phases that progressively lose their mobility, a process known as dynamic arrest. Biomolecular condensates, for example, become more viscous as they age,\cite{Jawerth2020-science} and some convert from liquid droplets into solid aggregates, a transition linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.\cite{Patel2015-cell,Alberti2021-nrmcb} Heat-set protein gels such as cooked egg white, and protein scaffolds that set at body temperature, follow a similar sequence: the proteins assemble first, and the network they form arrests later.\cite{Mezzenga2013-rpp,Begam2021-fh,Roberts2018-yh} In each case, the properties that matter, from the viscosity of a condensate to the stiffness of a gel, depend on how far and how fast the assembly has arrested. Assembly and arrest are, however, usually followed with different tools. Scattering and microscopy record structure, whereas mobility is measured by microrheology, bulk rheology and, for condensates, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching, each at its own length scale.\cite{Alberti2019-bp} Whether arrest simply tracks the growth of structure, and what sets its pace, have therefore been difficult to establish. The answers matter both for controlling when a protein material sets and for understanding how a condensate hardens.

Protein polymers whose assembly is encoded in their sequence are well suited to these questions, and elastin-like polypeptides (ELPs) are the best developed of them. ELPs are intrinsically disordered proteins whose sequence, molecular weight, and lower critical solution temperature (LCST) behavior can be programmed recombinantly,\cite{Meyer2004-zb,Amiram2011-la,Quiroz2015-mh,Varanko2020-ek,Saha2020-jg} and they have been proposed as minimal models of the disordered proteins that form condensates.\cite{Roberts2015-febs,Dzuricky2018-bk} Above the LCST, ELPs undergo hydrophobic desolvation and can form coacervates or hierarchical assemblies depending on sequence, concentration, and thermal history.\cite{Varanko2020-ek,Saha2020-jg,Sing2017-zq} In particular, alanine-containing ELPs can form stiff, thermoresponsive gels through arrested phase separation.\cite{Glassman2015-cw,Glassman2016-pq} Although these studies establish the arrested state from time-averaged scattering and rheology, how an assembly evolves toward arrest, and what paces it, remains elusive.

Following an assembly toward arrest requires measuring its structure and its dynamics at the same time, because the two can evolve on different time scales. Small-angle X-ray photon correlation spectroscopy (SA-XPCS) measures both simultaneously, on the same detector frames: averaging the frames in time gives the SAXS profile, and correlating them in time measures how fast that structure rearranges, at the same low-$Q$ length scales. Measuring them together places assembly and arrest in the same sample volume, at the same moment and on the same length scales, so the order and relative pace of the two can be read directly. In protein systems, XPCS has resolved two relaxation modes with ballistic droplet motion during liquid--liquid phase separation,\cite{Girelli2021-zz} temporally heterogeneous dynamics during gelation,\cite{Begam2021-fh} and stretched cage relaxation in crowded solutions.\cite{Chushkin2022-bw} A protein-rich morphology can likewise keep reorganizing through junction exchange, whose kinetics set the network relaxation time in associative networks,\cite{Sing2018-er} and through the residual coarsening that follows arrested phase separation.\cite{Tanaka2000-qq,Zhang2013-ja} Both can proceed while the SAXS profile changes only modestly.

Here we use SA-XPCS to follow assembly and arrest together in concentrated aqueous \VPAVG, an ELP built from 30 repeats of the pentapeptide valine-proline-alanine-valine-glycine. In isothermal experiments at 28.00, 28.25, 28.50 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}, the protein assembles at all four temperatures. The arrest that follows is strongly temperature dependent and sets in earlier at higher temperature. When the waiting time is rescaled with a single temperature scale, $T_s=0.24\pm0.02$~K, the arrest at all four temperatures falls onto one master curve: arrest speeds up by about a factor of three for every quarter of a degree of heating. The growth of structure seen by SAXS follows the same temperature-scaled clock. Arrest nonetheless trails assembly. By the time a sample is half arrested, most of its low-$Q$ scattering has already developed, and arrest continues while the SAXS intensity grows by less than a factor of two. This answers, for one protein polymer, the two questions raised at the outset: arrest trails assembly on a common clock, and temperature sets the pace of both. For protein materials, the time to arrest thus becomes a quantity that can be measured directly and tuned by sub-degree changes in temperature, and the steepness of its temperature scaling is a kinetic measure of how many contacts must form together in the step that sets it. For condensates, whose disordered proteins phase separate much as ELPs do, a cooperative step that paces assembly and arrest alike may determine when they turn from liquid to solid.

\section*{Results}
Fig.~\ref{fig:setup} summarizes the SA-XPCS experiment. All four loadings came from a single batch of \VPAVG{} at \SI{101}{\milli\gram\per\milli\liter} in \SI{20}{\milli\mole\per\liter} HEPES, \SI{250}{\milli\mole\per\liter} NaCl and \SI{2}{\milli\mole\per\liter} DTT at pH 8. Each \SI{2}{\second} SA-XPCS acquisition at Advanced Photon Source (APS) beamline 8-ID-I (Materials and Methods) recorded 100,000 detector frames. Averaging the frames in time and azimuth yields $I(Q)$, and multi-tau correlation of the same frames yields the intensity autocorrelation function
\begin{equation}
 g_2(Q,\Delta t)=\frac{\langle I(Q,t)I(Q,t+\Delta t)\rangle_t}{\langle I(Q,t)\rangle_t^2},
 \label{eq:g2}
\end{equation}
where $I(Q,t)$ is the coherent scattering intensity and $\Delta t$ is the delay time. Through the Siegert relation, $g_2$ reports the relaxation of density fluctuations at length scale $2\pi/Q$.

To resolve mesoscale arrest isothermally, each loading was equilibrated at \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius} for at least \SI{30}{\minute} and measured at that temperature. It was warmed from there to \SI{20}{\degreeCelsius} at \SI{1}{\degreeCelsius\per\minute} and heated at \SI{0.1}{\degreeCelsius\per\minute} to a final temperature between 28.00 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}, where it was held. The first ramp ended at \SI{20}{\degreeCelsius} because no appreciable assembly or dynamic arrest was observed at room temperature. The second ramp was kept slow to prevent the temperature from overshooting the target. The waiting time $t_w$ starts at the first acquisition with the calibrated resistance-temperature detector (RTD) at the cell reading the target. Acquisitions followed one another every \SI{6}{\second}, and consecutive acquisitions were averaged in groups of about 100, each spanning about \SI{10}{\minute}. Every $t_w$ quoted below is the midpoint of a group (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:tdepdata}).

Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs} follows the isothermal experiment at \SI{28.50}{\degreeCelsius} through the hour in which the sample arrests. It shows seven groups recorded between $t_w=300$ and \SI{3740}{\second}: the SAXS intensity (Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}a), the correlation function at the largest length scale (Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}b), and the parameters of the dynamics fitted at five length scales (Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}c). The isothermal experiments at the other three temperatures are shown in the same format in SI Appendix, Figs.~\ref*{SI-fig:T2800}, \ref*{SI-fig:T2825} and \ref*{SI-fig:T2875}. Radiation damage was controlled in two ways. Every acquisition used a previously unexposed sample position, so the cumulative dose does not grow with $t_w$, and the evolution in Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs} cannot be a dose artifact. In addition, a control at 22 to 41 times the flux of this experiment showed no change in the SAXS or in $g_2$ with dose rate (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:radiation} and Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:flux}). The three panels of Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs} support three observations at this temperature, which we take in turn. The protein assembles on heating, its dynamics arrest once the growth of the assembled structure has slowed, and a two-mode analysis of the correlation functions measures how far that arrest has gone at each length scale.

First of all, the protein assembles on heating, and its structure keeps growing at low $Q$ throughout the isothermal experiment (Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}a). At \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius} the background-subtracted intensity, on the absolute scale calibrated in SI Appendix, Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:calib}, is flat at about 0.03~mm$^{-1}$, as expected for dissolved protein. At \SI{28.50}{\degreeCelsius} it is two to three orders of magnitude higher at low $Q$. The profiles stay broad and monotonic with no peak emerging, so the assembly gains density contrast without settling on a single characteristic domain size. We follow its progress through the low-$Q$ intensity, the mean of $I(Q)$ over 0.004 to 0.008~\Ainv{} (shaded in Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}a). This band spans the length scales of the correlation analysis below, and it is where the intensity grows most: over the isothermal experiment, $I(Q)$ rises 5.2-fold at 0.004~\Ainv{} but only 2.1-fold at 0.02~\Ainv. In the first group at the target the low-$Q$ intensity is already 76 times its level at \SI{20}{\degreeCelsius}, and it grows fourfold more by the end of the isothermal experiment.

Secondly, arrest trails assembly: the dynamics arrest once the growth of the structure has slowed (Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}b). At the largest length scale, $Q=0.00359$~\Ainv{} (\SI{175}{\nano\meter}), the correlation functions decay in two steps: a relaxation on the millisecond scale, resolved only by the \SI{50}{\kilo\hertz} framing of the XSPA-500k,\cite{Nakaye2021-oc} followed by a plateau that persists through the \SI{2}{\second} acquisition. Such two-step relaxation, with a slow component that grows in time, is characteristic of heterogeneous kinetic arrest in protein and colloidal gels.\cite{Begam2021-fh,Bahadur2019-ln,Chen2020-fn} In the first two groups, at 300 and \SI{892}{\second}, the first step carries about 80\% of the decay, while the low-$Q$ intensity doubles from 3.1 to 6.1~mm$^{-1}$. In the groups that follow, the first step shrinks and the plateau rises toward $1+\beta$, the level of a sample that is fully arrested over the measured delays. Over these \SI{2850}{\second} the low-$Q$ intensity grows only 2.1-fold, to 12.7~mm$^{-1}$, having already reached 147 times its level at \SI{20}{\degreeCelsius} by \SI{892}{\second}.

Thirdly, a two-mode analysis of these correlation functions shows the arrested fraction growing at every length scale, first and most strongly at the largest, while the rate of the fast relaxation falls early and then levels off (Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}c). The two-step decays were fitted with
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 g_2(Q,\Delta t,t_w)&=1+\beta\left|g_1(Q,\Delta t,t_w)\right|^{2},\\
 g_1(Q,\Delta t,t_w)&=f(Q,t_w)\,g_{1,\mathrm{fast}}+\left[1-f(Q,t_w)\right]g_{1,\mathrm{slow}},\\
 g_{1,\mathrm{fast}}&=\exp\!\left[-\left(D_0Q^{2}\Delta t\right)^{p_{\mathrm{fast}}}\right],\\
 g_{1,\mathrm{slow}}&=\exp\!\left[-\left(\Delta t/\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}\right)^{p_{\mathrm{slow}}}\right].
\end{aligned}
 \label{eq:twomode}
\end{equation}
The first line is the Siegert relation, in which $g_1$ is the normalized intermediate scattering function and $\beta$ is the instrumental speckle contrast, measured on a static reference and held fixed (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:tdepbg}). The fast fraction $f$ is the share of the density fluctuations at $2\pi/Q$ that relax on the fast time scale. Its complement, $1-f$, is the share that stays arrested over the delays measured, so $f$ falls from near 1 toward 0 as a sample arrests. The fast relaxation time is written as $1/(D_0Q^2)$, the form expected if the material that is still mobile moves diffusively. At that delay the fast term has decayed to $1/\mathrm{e}$ of its amplitude, whatever the value of $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$, so $D_0$ measures the rate of the fast relaxation. The stretching exponents $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$ and $p_{\mathrm{slow}}$ measure how broad each relaxation is, $p=1$ being a single exponential. $\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}$ lies beyond the acquisition window and is an extrapolated descriptor, so the analysis rests on $f$ and $D_0$.

The fit uses the five lowest $Q$ bins, 0.00359 to 0.00756~\Ainv{} or 175 to \SI{83}{\nano\meter}; above them the scattered intensity has fallen by more than an order of magnitude and $g_2$ is dominated by counting noise. In each group, $f$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}$ are free in every bin, while $D_0$, $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$ and $p_{\mathrm{slow}}$ are shared across the five. $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$ is free from one group to the next because it falls from about 1 in a mobile sample to about 0.2 in an arrested one, and a single value shared across an isothermal experiment is rejected by an $F$ test (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:tdepfit}). The $Q^{-2}$ form of the fast time is imposed rather than fitted. Released from it, the fitted exponent has a median of $-2.5$, and the collapse of Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest} survives (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:fastscaling}).

With this model, Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}c shows the fast fraction falling at every measured length scale; the correlation functions and fits at the four higher $Q$ values are in SI Appendix, Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:T2850}. At $t_w=\SI{300}{\second}$, $f$ is 0.80 at \SI{175}{\nano\meter}; by \SI{3740}{\second} it has fallen to 0.07, so the arrested fraction $1-f$ has gone from 0.20 to 0.93 in under an hour. At every waiting time $f$ also increases monotonically with $Q$, in all seven groups, so arrest develops first and most strongly at the largest length scale probed, \SI{175}{\nano\meter}, and progressively less so toward \SI{83}{\nano\meter}. That ordering holds within each group, so it reflects length scale rather than the trend with $t_w$. It ties the growing low-$Q$ SAXS structure to the arrest measured on the very same frames.

The rate of the fast relaxation, shown as stars in Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}c, behaves differently from its amplitude. While the sample is still largely mobile, $D_0$ falls from 8.0 to $2.3\times10^{7}$~\AA$^2$\,s$^{-1}$. Once $f$ has dropped below 0.7, $D_0$ stays between 0.9 and $1.2\times10^{7}$~\AA$^2$\,s$^{-1}$ while $1-f$ climbs from 0.46 to 0.63, and the amplitude keeps falling, to 0.07, after the fast decay has become too weak to fix a rate (open stars). The longer isothermal experiment at \SI{28.25}{\degreeCelsius} shows the same over a wider range: the rate stays within a factor of 1.7, with no trend, while $f$ falls from 0.67 to 0.29 (SI Appendix, Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:fastmode}). The same pairing of a plateauing fast time scale with a declining fast amplitude was found in a thermoreversible nanoparticle gel.\cite{Bahadur2019-ln} The fast relaxation also broadens, $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$ falling from 0.7--0.9 at the start to 0.20--0.35 at the end of each isothermal experiment in which the sample arrests. At $p_{\mathrm{fast}}=1$ the fast term falls from 90 to 10\% of its amplitude over 1.3 decades of delay; at 0.2 it takes 6.7 decades. Late in arrest, therefore, the amplitude of the fast relaxation keeps falling, while its rate stays nearly constant and its decay spreads over a widening range of time scales.

Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest} extends the analysis to all four isothermal experiments, at 28.00, 28.25, 28.50 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}, and shows that one temperature-scaled clock paces both the arrest and the assembly. It follows $f$ at the largest length scale, $Q=0.00359$~\Ainv{} (\SI{175}{\nano\meter}), where arrest goes furthest, in all 34 fitted groups. Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}a plots this $f$ against $t_w$, Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}b plots it against a rescaled waiting time, and Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}c places the low-$Q$ intensity of the same isothermal experiments on that rescaled time.

To begin with, the rate of arrest depends steeply on temperature (Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}a). At \SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius}, $f$ stays at 0.89 or above throughout the \SI{6800}{\second} isothermal experiment, although the protein assembles (SI Appendix, Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:T2800}). At the three higher temperatures $f$ falls, and the fall sets in earlier at higher temperature: $f$ reaches 0.5 after 6100, 2220 and \SI{830}{\second} at 28.25, 28.50 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}, a 7.3-fold change over half a degree.

Next, one rescaling of the waiting time collapses the arrest at all four temperatures (Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}b). Against $X=t_w\exp[(T-T_0)/T_s]$, $f$ falls onto one master curve, $f=A/[1+(X/X_c)^n]$, with $T_s=0.24\pm0.02$~K. Collapsed one $Q$ bin at a time, $T_s$ reads 0.24 to 0.27~K, so the same temperature scale holds at every length scale measured, and the time scale of the master curve grows 1.3-fold from 175 to \SI{83}{\nano\meter} (SI Appendix, Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:collapse}). The collapse says more than that arrest is faster at higher temperature: one factor on the waiting time accounts for most of the difference between the four experiments, so temperature sets the pace of arrest. The \SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius} sample, which never arrests during its isothermal experiment, is the same curve seen too early: on the master curve it would be half arrested near \SI{17600}{\second}. A scaling temperature of 0.24~K means that arrest speeds up about threefold for every quarter of a degree of heating. A dependence this steep indicates a cooperative step, in which many contacts form together, as discussed below.

Finally, the assembly runs on the same clock (Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}c). Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}c places the low-$Q$ intensity of every group, fitted or not, on $t_w\exp[(T-T_0)/T_s]$ with the $T_s$ of Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}b. The intensities of all four isothermal experiments fall on one curve, and fitted on their own they return the same $T_s=0.24$~K. Other bands tested between 0.003 and 0.03~\Ainv{} superpose at 0.24 or 0.25~K (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:scaling}). Structure and arrest therefore advance on a common clock.

Along that clock, assembly runs ahead of arrest. To compare the two, we mark each sample as half arrested when $f$ at \SI{175}{\nano\meter} falls to 0.5, interpolated between the two groups that bracket it (stars in Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}c). At that point half of the density fluctuations at \SI{175}{\nano\meter} still relax on the fast time scale and half are arrested. It lies near the midpoint of the fall of $f$, and all three samples that arrest pass it. Each is half arrested when its low-$Q$ intensity reaches 7.2 to 8.3~mm$^{-1}$, 175 to 220 times its level at \SI{20}{\degreeCelsius}, and the intensity grows only 1.2- to 1.7-fold more while $f$ falls from 0.5 to between 0.07 and 0.16. The isothermal experiment at \SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius} ends at 5.8~mm$^{-1}$ with $f$ still 0.89, short of that stage. Other thresholds give the same picture: for any value between $f=0.3$ and 0.7, the samples cross it at 5.7 to 9.2~mm$^{-1}$, and the intensity grows 1.1- to 1.9-fold afterwards (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:scaling}). Arrest therefore follows assembly: a sample is half arrested only after most of its low-$Q$ structure has formed.

\section*{Discussion}
The Introduction asked whether the arrest of a protein assembly simply tracks the growth of its structure, and what sets its pace. Measuring structure and dynamics on the same frames answers both questions for \VPAVG. Arrest follows assembly: the low-$Q$ structure forms first, and the dynamics arrest as its growth slows. Both nonetheless advance on one clock, and temperature sets its pace. That clock is unusually steep, speeding up about threefold for every quarter of a degree of heating. Below we discuss what so steep a clock implies about the step that sets it, how that step fits with what is known about ELPs, and what it means for protein materials and condensates.

First, the steepness of the clock points to a cooperative step. Written as an Arrhenius rate, $k\propto\exp(-E_a/k_\mathrm{B}T)$, a scaling temperature of 0.24~K corresponds to an apparent activation energy $E_a=k_\mathrm{B}T^2/T_s\approx1.2\times10^{3}\,k_\mathrm{B}T$, or about \SI{3}{\mega\joule\per\mole}. An energy that large must be shared among many contacts. The same collapse was introduced for thermoreversible colloidal gels, where the formation rate varies with quench depth far more steeply than the pair attraction alone allows, and that steepness was attributed to the cooperative bonding needed to build locally favored structures.\cite{Zhang2017-ip,Chen2023-ho} In those gels, $T_s=0.33$ and 0.45~K for two particle sizes. Because the temperature dependence of the pair attraction was known, these values gave local structures held by about 26 and 19 bonds.\cite{Zhang2017-ip,Bahadur2019-ln} In a dense protein phase, whose contacts lie far below optical resolution, the scaling temperature is thus an in situ kinetic measure of how many contacts must form together.

Protein assembly gated by nucleation shows the same kind of steepness. The delay before deoxyhemoglobin S gels on heating varies as about the 30th power of protein concentration and almost halves for every degree of warming, which identified the nucleation of fibers as the rate-limiting step.\cite{Hofrichter1974-pnas} How steeply such a rate depends on concentration counts the molecules in the critical nucleus,\cite{Ferrone1999-me} and protein aggregation more generally accelerates once nucleation has started.\cite{Knowles2009-science} For an ELP, heating acts much like concentrating. ELPs assemble sharply once heated above their transition temperature,\cite{Amiram2011-la,Quiroz2015-mh} and at a fixed temperature a change in concentration can trigger the same transition.\cite{Meyer2004-zb,Roberts2015-febs} The arrest clock here speeds up about sixtyfold per degree, against twofold for sickle hemoglobin. For this protein, the temperature dependence of a single contact is not calibrated, so no bond number is extracted. The scaling still requires that arrest be gated by a step that forms many contacts at once.

Second, the step that sets the clock builds the mesoscale structure. Above the LCST, ELP assembly is driven by hydrophobic desolvation, and desolvation is well under way before any isothermal experiment begins: the low-$Q$ intensity has already grown two- to fourfold by \SI{27.5}{\degreeCelsius} on the ramp (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:tdep}). The equilibrium of a single contact shifts very little over half a degree, yet half a degree changes the rate of arrest sevenfold, and the low-$Q$ structure grows on the same clock. The rate-limiting event is therefore one that builds the mesoscale structure and then freezes it: the concerted formation of junctions in which many contacts close together.

Both mechanisms raised in the Introduction can supply such a step. In associative networks with ELP end-blocks, the end-blocks aggregate into junctions whose association and dissociation set the network relaxation time,\cite{Sing2018-er} so forming or breaking one junction moves a whole ELP block, many residues together. In a gel of associating coiled-coil proteins, tracer diffusion likewise suggests that clusters of chains move together on large length scales and detach from the network only when several coiled-coil domains dissociate.\cite{Rao2020-prr} In viscoelastic phase separation, a deep quench turns the slow, protein-rich phase into a transient gel and stalls its coarsening, and near a glass transition the mobility of that phase drops steeply.\cite{Tanaka2000-qq} Relaxation there requires many molecules to rearrange together.\cite{Adam1965-jcp} For an LCST protein, a deeper quench is a higher temperature. In both pictures, the step that sets the clock involves many contacts or molecules acting at once, which is what the steep temperature dependence requires.

Third, what is known about ELPs supports this picture. Individual contacts in an ELP coacervate are short-lived. NMR shows transient and nonspecific hydrophobic contacts between disordered chains,\cite{Reichheld2017-pnas} and simulations find the chains hydrated and highly disordered even as assembly triples their nonpolar side-chain contacts.\cite{Rauscher2017-elife} A structure that persists for hours must therefore be held by many such contacts at once. ELPs that carry alanine in the third position of the repeat, as \VPAVG{} does, are also known to arrest. Concentrated solutions of (XPAVG)$_n$ ELPs warmed above their transition temperature undergo arrested phase separation into stiff gels, and related ELPs form bicontinuous networks with stress-relaxation times beyond $10^3$~s.\cite{Glassman2015-cw,Glassman2016-pq} Alanine-containing ELP blocks also exchange between junctions more slowly than glycine-containing ones,\cite{Sing2018-er} and poly(VPAVG) loses much of its structural disorder as its coacervate matures.\cite{Amiram2011-la} The sequence observed here is consistent with network-forming arrested phase separation in a dynamically asymmetric protein--solvent mixture, where concentration fluctuations build a connected dense phase and viscoelastic constraints suppress relaxation.\cite{Tanaka2000-qq,Girelli2021-zz,Begam2021-fh,Lu2008-nature} These studies characterize the arrested state. The present data add how fast it is reached: arrest follows assembly, on a clock set by a cooperative step.

Finally, the steepness of the clock matters in practice. At $T_s=0.24$~K, a quarter of a degree of warming shortens the time to arrest by a factor of 2.8, so the rate at which an ELP network assembles and arrests is adjustable over orders of magnitude by sub-degree temperature control.

What paces arrest is also an open question for biomolecular condensates, whose maturation from liquid to solid governs their function and their role in aggregation.\cite{Banani2017-bb,Shin2017-hi} Condensates are too small and too dilute for the measurement reported here, but a concentrated, sequence-defined mimic is not. In this mimic, arrest follows assembly on a common clock, and the steep temperature dependence of that clock points to a cooperative step. Whether condensates harden through a step of the same kind can be tested with the tools already used on them: if they do, their arrest followed at a few temperatures should collapse under one rescaling of time.

Two limitations bound the interpretation, and each points to the measurement that would lift it. First, the \SIrange{2e-5}{1.6}{\second} delay range quantifies the fraction arrested on that scale rather than the infinite-time nonergodicity factor, for which linear shear rheology on a scaled-up preparation is the complement. Second, the scaling rests on three arresting temperatures spanning half a degree, and the collapse is approximate: rescaling improves the residual threefold but not to the measurement uncertainty. More temperatures would settle it. $T_0$ is a convention rather than a measured transition value, so no reference time is quoted from it.

Subject to those constraints, the measurements give one picture. SA-XPCS follows what SAXS alone cannot: how far an assembly has arrested. Structure and arrest run on one clock, with arrest following assembly and finishing after most of the low-$Q$ structure has formed. Arrest reaches the largest length scales first. One rescaling of time brings every isothermal experiment onto one master curve, so temperature sets the pace of arrest. That clock runs almost three times faster for every quarter of a degree of heating, a steepness that points to a rate-limiting step in which many contacts close together.

\section*{Materials and Methods}
The Sanger-verified construct encoded an N-terminal His-tagged \VPAVG{} protein (complete sequence in SI Appendix). Recombinant \VPAVG{} was expressed in \textit{E. coli}, purified by immobilized metal-affinity chromatography, and buffer exchanged into \SI{20}{\milli\mole\per\liter} HEPES, \SI{250}{\milli\mole\per\liter} NaCl, and \SI{2}{\milli\mole\per\liter} DTT at pH 8. Expression, solubility, and purity were monitored by SDS-PAGE (SI Appendix, Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:construct}b). Protein concentration was determined from $A_{280}$ on a NanoDrop spectrophotometer with $E^{1\%}_{280}=0.95$; the batch used for all four loadings was at \SI{101}{\milli\gram\per\milli\liter}.

SA-XPCS was performed at APS 8-ID-I with a \SI{10.94}{\kilo\electronvolt}, $10\times10~\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}$ partially coherent beam and a Rigaku XSPA-500k detector \SI{7.8}{\meter} downstream. Each acquisition contained 100,000 frames at \SI{50}{\kilo\hertz} (\SI{20}{\micro\second} per frame, \SI{2}{\second} total), and the four isothermal experiments were carried out in one beamtime in a single cell block. Further acquisition, fitting and radiation-control details are in SI Appendix.

The four loadings were measured one after another on the same temperature zone and resistance-temperature detector, in the order 28.00, 28.50, 28.25 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}, and the detector reading varied by \SI{0.004}{\degreeCelsius} (standard deviation) during each isothermal experiment. Acquisitions followed one another every \SI{6}{\second}, each at a fresh position. Consecutive acquisitions were averaged in groups of about 100 after two outlier cuts (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:coldstate}), and each group is placed at the midpoint of the acquisitions it contains, $t_w=0$ being the first acquisition with the detector at its target. Eq.~\ref{eq:twomode} was fitted to the five lowest $Q$ bins of each group at once by error-weighted least squares, with $\beta$ fixed at the value measured on a static reference, $f$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}$ free in each bin, and $D_0$, $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$ and $p_{\mathrm{slow}}$ shared across the bins of a group (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:b8xpcs}).

For the scaling analysis, $f$ at the lowest $Q$ of every group of the four isothermal experiments was fitted with one master curve, $f=A/[1+(X/X_c)^n]$ with $X=t_w\exp[(T-T_0)/T_s]$, weighting each point by its fit uncertainty floored at 0.01, and each higher $Q$ bin was collapsed the same way on its own. $T_0=\SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius}$ is a convention: it moves the curve along $X$ without changing $T_s$. The uncertainty of $T_s$ is the standard deviation over bootstrap resamples of whole groups. The low-$Q$ intensity is the mean absolute $I(Q)$ of each group over 0.004 to 0.008~\Ainv. A sample is half arrested at the waiting time at which $f$ at the lowest $Q$ falls to 0.5, interpolated linearly between the two groups that bracket it (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:scaling}).
