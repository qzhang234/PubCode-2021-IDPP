\begin{abstract}
Many proteins assemble into dense phases that later lose their mobility, as when a condensate hardens or a protein gel sets. Assembly and arrest are usually followed with different tools, so it has been difficult to establish what sets the pace of arrest and whether the growth of structure keeps pace with it. Small-angle X-ray photon correlation spectroscopy (SA-XPCS) records both structure and dynamics on the same detector frames. We apply it to the elastin-like polypeptide \VPAVG{}, which assembles in isothermal experiments at four temperatures between 28.00 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}. Its correlation functions decay in two steps, and the amplitude of the fast step falls as the sample arrests. At \SI{28.50}{\degreeCelsius}, this amplitude drops from 0.80 to 0.07 at \SI{175}{\nano\meter} within an hour, and less far at smaller length scales down to \SI{83}{\nano\meter}. One rescaling of the waiting time, with a scaling temperature $T_s=0.24\pm0.02$~K, collapses the arrest at all four temperatures onto a single master curve. Arrest speeds up by about a factor of three for every quarter of a degree of heating. The low-$Q$ SAXS intensity follows the same clock. Along that clock, arrest trails assembly: in each sample that arrests, the fast-step amplitude falls to one half only after most of the low-$Q$ scattering has developed. The clock's activation enthalpy, 3.1~MJ/mol, is 6 to 14 times that of the calorimetric transition of poly(VPAVG). Arrest is therefore set by an additional barrier, of another origin or from a step that joins several chains.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Significance Statement}
Many proteins assemble into dense phases that later stop moving, as when a condensate hardens or a gel sets. Structure and motion are usually measured separately, so it has been hard to tell what sets the pace at which motion stops and whether structure keeps that pace. We record both at once in a designed protein polymer with fast X-ray scattering. Both run on one clock, and the motion stops after most of the structure has formed. The clock runs about three times faster for every quarter of a degree of heating, so a fraction of a degree decides when this protein material sets. This clock is far steeper than the polymer's calorimetric transition, so desolvation alone cannot set it.


\section*{Introduction}
Many proteins assemble into dense phases that progressively lose their mobility, a process known as dynamic arrest. For example, biomolecular condensates become more viscous as they age,\cite{Jawerth2020-science} and some convert from liquid droplets into solid aggregates, a transition linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis.\cite{Patel2015-cell,Alberti2021-nrmcb} Heat-set protein gels,\cite{Mezzenga2013-rpp} such as cooked egg white\cite{Begam2021-fh,Timmermann2023-sr} and protein scaffolds that set at body temperature,\cite{Roberts2018-yh} follow a similar sequence: the proteins assemble first, and the network they form arrests later. In each case, the properties that matter, from the viscosity of a condensate to the stiffness of a gel, depend on how far and how fast the assembly has arrested. In heat-set gels, temperature sets that pace by unfolding the proteins. Heat-induced aggregation of food proteins follows an Arrhenius law,\cite{Mezzenga2013-rpp} and coherent X-ray measurements on heated egg yolk show gelation following an Arrhenius clock whose activation energy matches that of protein denaturation.\cite{Anthuparambil2023-nc} Many of the proteins that form condensates are wholly or partly disordered,\cite{Banani2017-bb} and a disordered chain has no folded structure to unfold. Three questions about such proteins have been difficult to answer: what sets the pace of their arrest, whether their structure grows at the same pace, and what the temperature dependence of that pace reveals about the limiting step. One reason is that assembly and arrest are usually followed with different tools. Scattering and microscopy record structure, whereas mobility is measured by microrheology and bulk rheology, and in condensates by fluorescence recovery after photobleaching, each at its own length scale.\cite{Alberti2019-bp} The answers matter both for controlling when a protein material sets and for understanding how a condensate hardens.

Protein polymers whose assembly is encoded in their sequence are well suited to these questions, and elastin-like polypeptides (ELPs) are the best developed of them. ELPs are intrinsically disordered proteins whose sequence, molecular weight and lower critical solution temperature (LCST) behavior can be programmed recombinantly,\cite{Meyer2004-zb,Amiram2011-la,Quiroz2015-mh,Varanko2020-ek,Saha2020-jg} and they have been proposed as minimal models of the disordered proteins that form condensates.\cite{Roberts2015-febs,Dzuricky2018-bk} Because an ELP is a single sequence-defined chain that assembles on heating without unfolding, it isolates the case in which temperature acts on contacts rather than on folds. Above the LCST, ELPs undergo hydrophobic desolvation and can form coacervates or hierarchical assemblies depending on sequence, concentration and thermal history.\cite{Varanko2020-ek,Saha2020-jg,Sing2017-zq} In particular, alanine-containing ELPs can form stiff, thermoresponsive gels through arrested phase separation.\cite{Glassman2015-cw,Glassman2016-pq} Although these studies establish the arrested state from time-averaged scattering and rheology, it remains unclear how an assembly evolves toward arrest and what paces it.

Following an assembly toward arrest requires measuring its structure and its dynamics at the same time, because the two can evolve on different time scales. Small-angle X-ray photon correlation spectroscopy (SA-XPCS) measures both on the same detector frames: averaging the frames in time gives the SAXS profile, and correlating them in time measures how fast that structure rearranges, at the same low-$Q$ length scales. Measuring them together places assembly and arrest in the same sample volume, at the same moment and on the same length scales, so the relative pace and the order of the two can be read directly. In protein systems, XPCS has resolved two relaxation modes with ballistic droplet motion during liquid--liquid phase separation,\cite{Girelli2021-zz} temporally heterogeneous dynamics during gelation,\cite{Begam2021-fh} and stretched cage relaxation in crowded solutions.\cite{Chushkin2022-bw} A protein-rich assembly can also keep reorganizing while its SAXS profile changes only modestly,\cite{Sing2018-er,Tanaka2000-qq} so its arrest can be followed only through its dynamics.

Here we use isothermal SA-XPCS to follow assembly and arrest together in concentrated aqueous \VPAVG, an ELP built from 30 repeats of the pentapeptide valine-proline-alanine-valine-glycine. Assembly and arrest are recorded as functions of the waiting time $t_w$ at 28.00, 28.25, 28.50 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}. We find that the time scales of assembly and arrest shorten exponentially with increasing temperature and share one scaling temperature, and that arrest follows assembly along this common clock. The scaling temperature corresponds to an Arrhenius activation enthalpy of 3.1~MJ/mol, about an order of magnitude larger than that of the calorimetric transition of poly(VPAVG). The difference suggests that the step that sets the pace of arrest is not desolvation alone but either joins several chains or has another origin. Our work demonstrates that the activation enthalpy of assembly and arrest can be measured directly in a disordered protein of the kind that forms condensates and compared with calorimetry. Combined with the tunable sequence of ELPs, this approach offers a new way to study the molecular origin of the barrier to structural assembly and dynamic arrest in biomacromolecules.

\section*{Results}
Fig.~\ref{fig:setup} summarizes the isothermal SA-XPCS experiment at Advanced Photon Source (APS) beamline 8-ID-I. A partially coherent X-ray beam with a photon energy of \SI{10.94}{\kilo\electronvolt} and an incident flux of $1.2\times10^{10}$ photons per second was focused to a $10\times10~\mu\mathrm{m}^2$ spot on the sample. The sample filled a \SI{2}{\milli\meter}-thick, \SI{3}{\milli\meter}-diameter chamber in an aluminum cell, sealed by \SI{0.127}{\milli\meter} polycarbonate windows. Four temperatures were chosen: 28.00, 28.25, 28.50 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}. Each temperature used a fresh aliquot of \VPAVG{} at \SI{101}{\milli\gram\per\milli\liter} from the same batch (SI Appendix, Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:construct}). Each loaded cell was kept in a \SI{4}{\degreeCelsius} refrigerator until it was placed in the temperature-controlled stage and measured at \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius}. From there it was heated to \SI{20}{\degreeCelsius} at \SI{1}{\degreeCelsius\per\minute} and to its target temperature at \SI{0.1}{\degreeCelsius\per\minute}. 

SA-XPCS measurements were taken at \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius} and continuously from the moment the sample reached \SI{20}{\degreeCelsius}. The waiting time $t_w$ counts from the first acquisition at which the stage temperature reached the target. Each SA-XPCS acquisition consisted of 100,000 frames of \SI{20}{\micro\second} collected with a Rigaku XSPA-500k pixelated photon-counting detector \SI{7.8}{\meter} downstream.\cite{Zhang2021-jl,Nakaye2021-oc} The \SI{2}{\second} acquisitions were repeated approximately every \SI{6}{\second}, each at a fresh spot on the sample to avoid beam damage. Averaging the frames in time and azimuth yields $I(Q)$, and correlating the same frames in time yields the intensity autocorrelation function
\begin{equation}
 g_2(Q,\Delta t)=\frac{\langle I(Q,t)I(Q,t+\Delta t)\rangle_t}{\langle I(Q,t)\rangle_t^2},
 \label{eq:g2}
\end{equation}
where $I(Q,t)$ and $I(Q,t+\Delta t)$ are the coherent scattering intensities recorded by a pixel at momentum transfer $Q$ in frames $t$ and $t+\Delta t$, and $\Delta t$ is the delay time between the two frames. The statistics of $g_2$ improve through two averages: the time average $\langle\cdot\rangle_t$ over all pairs of frames separated by $\Delta t$, and the average over all pixels in the same $Q$ bin. Through the Siegert relation, $g_2$ reports the relaxation of density fluctuations at length scale $2\pi/Q$. Details regarding the measurement protocol and data reduction can be found in Materials and Methods.

Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs} shows the isothermal SA-XPCS results at \SI{28.50}{\degreeCelsius} up to $t_w=\SI{3740}{\second}$. Each data point averages a group of about 100 consecutive acquisitions recorded over about \SI{10}{\minute} to improve the $g_2$ statistics, with $t_w$ placed at the midpoint of the group. The SAXS intensity shows that the protein assembles on heating and keeps building structure at low $Q$ throughout the isothermal experiment (Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}a). Details regarding the conversion of the raw detector counts to absolute units can be found in SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:b8saxs} and Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:calib}. At \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius} the intensity is mostly flat over the entire observable $Q$ range, 0.0036 to 0.03~\Ainv, as expected for dissolved protein, whereas at \SI{28.50}{\degreeCelsius} it is two to three orders of magnitude higher at the low-$Q$ limit. The profiles stay broad and monotonic with no peak emerging, so the assembly gains density contrast without settling on a single characteristic domain size. We follow its progress through the low-$Q$ intensity, the mean of $I(Q)$ over 0.004 to 0.008~\Ainv{} (shaded in Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}a), a band that spans the length scales of the correlation analysis and in which the intensity grows most. Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest} compares its growth with waiting time across the four temperatures.

The correlation functions reveal that arrest trails assembly: the dynamics arrest once the growth of the structure has slowed (Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}b). At the largest length scale, $Q=0.00359$~\Ainv{} (\SI{175}{\nano\meter}), $g_2$ decays in two steps: a relaxation on the millisecond scale, followed by a plateau that persists through the \SI{2}{\second} acquisition. Such two-step relaxation with a slow component that grows in time is characteristic of heterogeneous kinetic arrest in protein and colloidal gels.\cite{Begam2021-fh,Bahadur2019-ln,Chen2020-fn} A radiation-damage test at 22 to 41 times the incident flux showed no change in the SAXS or in $g_2$ (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:radiation} and Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:flux}). All four isothermal experiments are shown in the same format in SI Appendix, Figs.~\ref*{SI-fig:T2800}--\ref*{SI-fig:T2875}, where the two-step decay can be seen at $Q$ up to 0.00756~\Ainv. In the first two groups, at 294 and \SI{886}{\second}, the fast step carries about 80\% of the decay while the low-$Q$ intensity doubles. In the groups that follow, the fast step shrinks and the plateau rises toward $1+\beta$, the level of a sample fully arrested over the measured delays. Meanwhile the low-$Q$ intensity grows only 2.1-fold more.

A two-mode analysis of these correlation functions shows the arrested fraction growing at every length scale, first and most strongly at the largest, while the rate of the fast relaxation falls early and then levels off (Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}c). The two-step decays were fitted with
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 g_2(Q,\Delta t,t_w)&=1+\beta\left|g_1(Q,\Delta t,t_w)\right|^{2},\\
 g_1(Q,\Delta t,t_w)&=f(Q,t_w)\,g_{1,\mathrm{fast}}+\left[1-f(Q,t_w)\right]g_{1,\mathrm{slow}},\\
 g_{1,\mathrm{fast}}&=\exp\!\left[-\left(D_0Q^{2}\Delta t\right)^{p_{\mathrm{fast}}}\right],\\
 g_{1,\mathrm{slow}}&=\exp\!\left[-\left(\Delta t/\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}\right)^{p_{\mathrm{slow}}}\right].
\end{aligned}
 \label{eq:twomode}
\end{equation}
The first line is the Siegert relation, in which $g_1$ is the normalized intermediate scattering function and $\beta$ is the speckle contrast, measured on a static reference. The fast fraction $f$ is the share of the density fluctuations at $2\pi/Q$ that relax on the fast time scale. Its complement, $1-f$, is the share that stays arrested over the delays measured, so $f$ falls from near 1 toward 0 as a sample arrests. The fast relaxation time is written as $1/(D_0Q^2)$, the form expected for diffusion. At that delay the fast term has decayed to $1/\mathrm{e}$ of its amplitude whatever the value of $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$, so $D_0$ measures the rate of the fast relaxation. The stretching exponents $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$ and $p_{\mathrm{slow}}$ measure how broad each relaxation is, $p=1$ being a single exponential. Because $\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}$ lies beyond the acquisition window, the analysis rests on $f$ and $D_0$, fitted in each group at the five lowest $Q$ values, 175 to \SI{83}{\nano\meter} (Materials and Methods).

With this model, the fast fraction falls at every measured length scale (Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}c; correlation functions and fits at the four higher $Q$ values in SI Appendix, Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:T2850}). At \SI{175}{\nano\meter}, $f$ falls from 0.80 at $t_w=\SI{294}{\second}$ to 0.07 at \SI{3740}{\second}, so the arrested fraction $1-f$ grows from 0.20 to 0.93 in under an hour. In all seven groups $f$ also increases monotonically with $Q$, so arrest develops first and most strongly at \SI{175}{\nano\meter} and progressively less so toward \SI{83}{\nano\meter}. Because this ordering holds within each group, it reflects length scale rather than the trend with $t_w$, and it ties the growing low-$Q$ structure to the arrest measured on the very same frames.

The rate of the fast relaxation behaves differently from its amplitude (stars in Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}c). While the sample is still largely mobile, $D_0$ falls from 8.0 to $2.3\times10^{7}$~\AA$^2$\,s$^{-1}$. Once $f$ has dropped below 0.7, $D_0$ stays between 0.9 and $1.2\times10^{7}$~\AA$^2$\,s$^{-1}$ while the amplitude keeps falling. The same pairing of a nearly constant rate with a falling amplitude appears over a wider range at \SI{28.25}{\degreeCelsius} (SI Appendix, Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:fastmode}) and was found in a thermoreversible nanoparticle gel.\cite{Bahadur2019-ln} The fast relaxation also broadens, $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$ falling from 0.7--0.9 at the start to 0.20--0.35 at the end of each isothermal experiment in which the sample arrests. Late in arrest the amplitude of the fast relaxation therefore keeps falling, while its rate stays nearly constant and its decay spreads over a widening range of time scales.

Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs} establishes that arrest trails assembly at one temperature, but not what sets its pace. To find out, Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest} compares the isothermal experiments at 28.00, 28.25, 28.50 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}. It plots $f$ at \SI{175}{\nano\meter}, where arrest goes furthest, for all 34 fitted groups against $t_w$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}a) and against a rescaled waiting time (Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}b). It also places the low-$Q$ intensity of the same experiments on that rescaled time (Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}c). The three panels show that one temperature-scaled clock paces both the arrest and the assembly.

The rate of arrest depends steeply on temperature (Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}a). At \SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius}, $f$ stays at 0.89 or above up to $t_w=\SI{6820}{\second}$, although the protein assembles (SI Appendix, Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:T2800}). At the three higher temperatures $f$ falls, and the fall sets in earlier at higher temperature: $f$ reaches 0.5 after 6090, 2220 and \SI{825}{\second} at 28.25, 28.50 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}, a 7.4-fold change over half a degree. Each quarter-degree step shortens that time by nearly the same factor, about 2.7, which is what a single temperature scale predicts.

One rescaling of the waiting time collapses the arrest at all four temperatures (Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}b). Against $X=t_w\exp[(T-T_0)/T_s]$, $f$ falls onto one master curve, $f=A/[1+(X/X_c)^n]$, with $T_s=0.24\pm0.02$~K. The same exponential rescaling of the waiting time describes the formation of thermoreversible colloidal gels.\cite{Zhang2017-ip} Collapsed one $Q$ bin at a time, $T_s$ reads 0.24 to 0.27~K, so the same temperature scale holds at every length scale measured (SI Appendix, Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:collapse}). The collapse says more than that arrest is faster at higher temperature: one factor on the waiting time accounts for most of the difference between the four experiments, so temperature sets the pace of arrest. The \SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius} sample, which never arrests during its isothermal experiment, is the same curve seen too early: on the master curve its $f$ would reach 0.5 near \SI{17600}{\second}. A scaling temperature of 0.24~K means that arrest speeds up about threefold for every quarter of a degree of heating.

The assembly runs on the same clock as the arrest (Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}c). Placed on $t_w\exp[(T-T_0)/T_s]$ with the $T_s$ of Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}b, the low-$Q$ intensities of all four isothermal experiments fall on one curve, and fitted on their own they return the same $T_s=0.24$~K. Other bands between 0.003 and 0.03~\Ainv{} superpose at 0.24 or 0.25~K (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:scaling}). Structure and arrest therefore advance on a common clock.

Along that clock, assembly runs ahead of arrest. We mark each sample as half arrested when $f$ at \SI{175}{\nano\meter} falls to 0.5 (stars in Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}c), the point at which half of the density fluctuations at that length scale still relax on the fast time scale. The three samples that arrest reach it when their low-$Q$ intensity is 7.2 to 8.3~mm$^{-1}$, 177 to 209 times its level at \SI{20}{\degreeCelsius}. From there the intensity grows only 1.2- to 1.7-fold more while $f$ falls to between 0.07 and 0.16. The sample at \SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius} ends at 5.8~mm$^{-1}$ with $f$ still 0.89, short of that stage. Any threshold between $f=0.3$ and 0.7 gives the same picture (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:scaling}). Arrest therefore follows assembly: a sample is half arrested only after most of its low-$Q$ structure has formed.

The rescaling also gives the activation energy of arrest, $E_a$. The collapse of $f$ at each $Q$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest}b and SI Appendix, Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:collapse}) indicates that the waiting time to reach any given $f$ varies as $\exp[-(T-T_0)/T_s]$, so the rate of arrest $k$ varies as $\exp[(T-T_0)/T_s]$ and $\mathrm{d}\ln k/\mathrm{d}T=1/T_s$. For an Arrhenius rate, $k\propto\exp(-E_a/k_\mathrm{B}T)$ and $\mathrm{d}\ln k/\mathrm{d}T=E_a/k_\mathrm{B}T^2$. Equating the two slopes at the temperature of the experiments gives
\begin{equation}
 E_a=\frac{k_\mathrm{B}T^2}{T_s}.
 \label{eq:ea}
\end{equation}
At the mean temperature of the four experiments, $T=301.5$~K, $T_s=0.24\pm0.02$~K gives $E_a=(1.2\pm0.1)\times10^{3}\,k_\mathrm{B}T$, or $3.1\pm0.3$~MJ/mol (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:bondnumber}). A similar Arrhenius dependence, with smaller activation energies, has been found in heated egg yolk: $450\pm20$~kJ/mol for its gelation and $410\pm20$~kJ/mol for its lipoprotein aggregation.\cite{Anthuparambil2023-nc} Such large values do not imply an impassable barrier. In transition-state theory the rate of a step is set by the free energy of activation, $\Delta G^\ddagger=\Delta H^\ddagger-T\Delta S^\ddagger$, and here a large entropy of activation $\Delta S^\ddagger$ offsets most of the enthalpy of activation $\Delta H^\ddagger=E_a-k_\mathrm{B}T$ (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:bondnumber}). This differs from the case in which the Arrhenius slope comes from the relaxation time between states of similar energy and entropy, as in solid-state systems that fluctuate between such states, where $E_a$ ranges from 34 to 283~kJ/mol.\cite{Zhang2017-oz,Ruta2014-nc,Stana2013-cm} Further evidence comes from the fast relaxation time at \SI{175}{\nano\meter}, $1/(D_0Q^2)$. Compared at the same stage of arrest, it stays between 1 and 3~ms at all four temperatures, while the waiting time needed to reach that stage ranges from \SI{6600}{\second} at \SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius} to \SI{300}{\second} at \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}. This suggests that different mechanisms govern the relaxation between states of similar energy and the transition between states of different free energy.

\section*{Discussion}
The activation energy $E_a$ of arrest from XPCS measures the enthalpy difference between the starting state and the transition state of the rate-limiting step during the arrest. The same quantity can also be obtained from the calorimetric transition of poly(VPAVG), in which each pentapeptide desolvates and forms contacts. However, these two measurements yield very different values: 3.1~MJ/mol for arrest and 220 to 480~kJ/mol for the calorimetric transition in water\cite{Reguera2003-ma} (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:bondnumber}). Two scenarios could explain this discrepancy. In scenario A, the barrier is built from the transition enthalpy that calorimetry records as heat, carried by many repeats at once. Because an activation energy is counted per rate-limiting event and the transition enthalpy per repeat, $E_a$ divided by the transition enthalpy of one repeat then counts how many repeats change state together in one event. In scenario B, the barrier to arrest has an origin other than that transition enthalpy, so $E_a$ is a property of the path the assembly takes.

In scenario A, each repeat that changes state in the rate-limiting step adds its transition enthalpy $\Delta H$ to the activation enthalpy, so the step involves $N\approx E_a/\Delta H$ repeats. This enthalpy is the net of desolvation, which absorbs heat, and of the contacts that replace the water, which release it.\cite{Girotti2004-ma,Ribeiro2009-bj} The count therefore assumes that each repeat at the top of the barrier carries this full net value. Scaled to the 30 repeats of this protein, the calorimetric $\Delta H$ of poly(VPAVG) is 7.2 to 9.5~kJ/mol per pentapeptide,\cite{Luan1991-bp,Reguera2003-ma,Ribeiro2009-bj,Girotti2004-ma} so $N\approx330$ to 440, the content of 11 to 15 chains (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:bondnumber}). Without this scaling $N$ would be 220 to 270. Either way the step joins several chains, and because the calorimetric heat follows a step with a smaller activation energy, this multichain step would carry only a small share of that heat. What is known about ELP contacts is consistent with such a step: the contacts are transient and nonspecific,\cite{Reichheld2017-pnas} and the chains stay hydrated and disordered as assembly proceeds,\cite{Rauscher2017-elife} so a structure that persists for hours needs many contacts at once. Two multichain steps fit the data. As in nucleation-limited protein gelation,\cite{Hofrichter1974-pnas,Ferrone1999-me} a critical nucleus of several chains would make the rate at fixed temperature rise with a power of concentration close to its number of chains.\cite{Ford1997-pre} Junctions that join several chains and accumulate until they connect would make the sample stiffen as $f$ falls, which shear rheology can test. The common low-$Q$ intensity at half arrest already fits this picture.

On the other hand, scenario B includes steps that slow the dense phase through barriers not built from the calorimetric enthalpy. Such steps can also involve many chains at once, but their size cannot then be read from $E_a$. One candidate is arrested phase separation, in which the dense phase slows steeply as it concentrates toward a gel or glass, as proposed for gels of related alanine-containing ELPs.\cite{Glassman2015-cw} Arrested spinodal decomposition would leave a scattering peak at its characteristic spacing; the profiles here show none between 20 and \SI{175}{\nano\meter}, so any such spacing would have to be larger, which ultra-small-angle scattering could test. Another candidate is conformational ordering inside the dense phase. Poly(VPAVG) loses much of its conformational disorder as its coacervate matures,\cite{Amiram2011-la} and spectroscopy of the dense phase during the isothermal experiments would show whether that ordering follows the clock.

Three limitations bound these conclusions, and each points to the measurement that would lift it. First, the \SIrange{2e-5}{1.6}{\second} delay range quantifies the fraction arrested on that scale rather than the infinite-time nonergodicity factor, for which linear shear rheology on a scaled-up preparation is the complement. Second, the scaling rests on three arresting temperatures spanning half a degree, and the collapse is approximate: rescaling improves the residual threefold but not to the measurement uncertainty. A wider range of temperatures would test the collapse and show how far this clock extends. Because $T_0$ is a convention rather than a measured transition value, no reference time is quoted from it. Third, the calorimetry comes from other chains in water, and comparing it with $E_a$ cannot separate scenario A from B. Calorimetry of the construct itself, in its buffer and at several heating rates, would give its transition enthalpy and the activation energy of its transition directly. Isothermal calorimetry at the temperatures used here, rescaled in the same way as the XPCS, would show whether the heat follows the clock. Under A, $T_s$ should vary inversely with the enthalpy of the units that cooperate, so it should move accordingly when that enthalpy is changed in a known way through chain length, the guest residue or salt; under B it need not. Shorter chains of the same sequence would also show whether the step joins a fixed number of pentapeptides or of whole chains, and block chains that place the VPAVG repeats at the ends or in the middle would show where the contacts form. Such tests need a monodisperse protein whose length and sequence can be set one repeat at a time, which recombinant expression provides.

Taken together, the measurements show that one clock, with an activation energy of about 3~MJ/mol, governs both the growth and the arrest of this protein assembly, and that the step setting it is not the desolvation that dominates the calorimetric heat of the polymer. At $T_s=0.24$~K, a quarter of a degree of warming shortens the time to arrest by a factor of 2.8, so a fraction of a degree decides when such a material arrests. Measuring structure and dynamics together, and comparing their clock with calorimetry of the same protein, can show what kind of step paces the setting of a protein material, and perhaps the hardening of a condensate.

\section*{Materials and Methods}
The Sanger-verified construct encoded an N-terminal His-tagged \VPAVG{} protein (complete sequence in SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:synthesis}). Recombinant \VPAVG{} was expressed in \textit{E. coli}, purified by immobilized metal-affinity chromatography, and buffer exchanged into \SI{20}{\milli\mole\per\liter} HEPES, \SI{250}{\milli\mole\per\liter} NaCl and \SI{2}{\milli\mole\per\liter} DTT at pH 8. Expression, solubility and purity were monitored by SDS-PAGE (SI Appendix, Fig.~\ref*{SI-fig:construct}b). Protein concentration was determined from $A_{280}$ on a NanoDrop spectrophotometer with $E^{1\%}_{280}=0.95$; the batch used for all four loadings was at \SI{101}{\milli\gram\per\milli\liter}.

The \SI{20}{\micro\second} frame time resolves the millisecond relaxation of Fig.~\ref{fig:xpcs}b. The four isothermal experiments were carried out in one beamtime in a single cell block. Further acquisition, fitting and radiation-control details are in SI Appendix.

Each loading was also measured at \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius} before heating. The \SI{1}{\degreeCelsius\per\minute} ramp ended at \SI{20}{\degreeCelsius} because no appreciable assembly or dynamic arrest was observed at room temperature, and the \SI{0.1}{\degreeCelsius\per\minute} ramp was kept slow to prevent the temperature from overshooting the target. The four loadings were measured one after another on the same temperature zone and resistance-temperature detector (RTD), in the order 28.00, 28.50, 28.25 and \SI{28.75}{\degreeCelsius}, and the RTD reading varied by \SI{0.004}{\degreeCelsius} (standard deviation) during each isothermal experiment (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:tdepdata}).

Two outlier cuts were applied before the acquisitions were averaged into groups (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:coldstate}). $I(Q)$ was put on an absolute scale through ion-chamber monitors calibrated against a PIN diode, and a measurement of the empty cell, scaled by a coefficient set from the \SI{6}{\degreeCelsius} references, was subtracted as background (SI Appendix, sections~\ref*{SI-sec:b8saxs} and \ref*{SI-sec:tdepbg}).

Eq.~\ref{eq:twomode} was fitted to the five lowest $Q$ bins of each group at once, 0.00359 to 0.00756~\Ainv{} (175 to \SI{83}{\nano\meter}), by error-weighted least squares. Above these bins the scattered intensity falls by more than an order of magnitude and $g_2$ is dominated by counting noise. The speckle contrast was fixed at $\beta=0.141$, measured on a static reference (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:tdepbg}), with $f$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{slow}}$ free in each bin and $D_0$, $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$ and $p_{\mathrm{slow}}$ shared across the bins of a group (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:b8xpcs}). $p_{\mathrm{fast}}$ is free from one group to the next because it falls from about 1 in a mobile sample to about 0.2 in an arrested one, and a single value shared across an isothermal experiment is rejected by an $F$ test (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:tdepfit}). The $Q^{-2}$ form of the fast time is imposed rather than fitted. Released from it, the fitted exponent has a median of $-2.5$, and the collapse of Fig.~\ref{fig:arrest} survives (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:fastscaling}). Parameter uncertainties are one standard deviation, from the covariance matrix of the weighted fit.

For the scaling analysis, $f$ at the lowest $Q$ of every group of the four isothermal experiments was fitted with one master curve, $f=A/[1+(X/X_c)^n]$ with $X=t_w\exp[(T-T_0)/T_s]$, weighting each point by its fit uncertainty floored at 0.01, and each higher $Q$ bin was collapsed the same way on its own. $T_0=\SI{28.00}{\degreeCelsius}$ is a convention: it moves the curve along $X$ without changing $T_s$. The uncertainty of $T_s$ is the standard deviation over bootstrap resamples of whole groups. The low-$Q$ intensity is the mean absolute $I(Q)$ of each group over 0.004 to 0.008~\Ainv. A sample is half arrested at the waiting time at which $f$ at the lowest $Q$ falls to 0.5, interpolated linearly between the two groups that bracket it (SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:scaling}). The activation energy, the free energy of activation and the collective bond number follow from $T_s$, the half-arrest times and published calorimetric enthalpies, with the length interpolation described in SI Appendix, section~\ref*{SI-sec:bondnumber}.
